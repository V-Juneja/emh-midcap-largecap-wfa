Abstract

1. Introduction

The Efficient Market Hypothesis, introduced by Fama (1970), affirms that asset prices must incorporate all
available information at any given time. This effectively dismisses the existence of any technical strategy
capable of generating long-term excess returns and challenges the theoretical basis of statistical arbitrage.
Yet markets are not purely mechanical — asset prices exhibit excess volatility inconsistent with what
fundamental values alone would justify (Shiller, 1981), and in practice it is near impossible for prices to
reflect all available information at any given moment. This disconnect between theory and observed market
behaviour has motivated a substantial body of research questioning the universality of the EMH, particularly
in markets characterised by information asymmetry, limited institutional oversight, and high retail
participation. Large-cap equities generally benefit from extensive analyst coverage and significant
institutional footprint, making them broadly considered to be semi-strong-form efficient (Fama, 1991). Mid-
cap equities, by contrast, receive far less media and institutional attention, rendering them comparatively
less efficient and therefore more susceptible to mispricing and slower market corrections.
Mean reversion is a technical approach that relies on the tendency of asset prices to return to their historical
mean following a period of deviation. The theoretical underpinning of this phenomenon lies in the
assumption that price deviations from intrinsic value are temporary — driven by overreaction, sentiment, or
liquidity shocks — and that rational market forces will eventually correct them. In large-cap equities, such
deviations may be smaller and corrections faster, owing to their higher efficiency and the speed at which
new information is priced in by institutional participants. Mid-cap equities, however, exhibit slower price
correction mechanisms due to lower liquidity and higher retail participation — conditions that behavioural
finance research has consistently linked to persistent mispricing and predictable return patterns (De Bondt &amp;
Thaler, 1985). This asymmetry suggests that mean reversion strategies should generate superior risk-
adjusted returns in mid-cap environments relative to large-caps, and that the differential itself serves as a
measurable proxy for market efficiency.
The Indian equity market provides a particularly compelling setting for this analysis. As one of the fastest-
growing emerging markets in the world, with over 100 million registered investors as of 2023 (SEBI, 2023)
and rising, India has seen rapid expansion in both retail and institutional participation over the past decade.
Despite this growth, market efficiency remains deeply uneven across segments. The NIFTY 100, comprising
the largest and most liquid Indian equities, attracts significant institutional coverage, analyst scrutiny, and

high-frequency trading activity — all of which accelerate price discovery. The NIFTY Midcap 150, by
contrast, remains comparatively under-researched and under-institutionalised. A larger proportion of trading
in this segment is driven by retail investors, who are more susceptible to behavioural biases, making it a
natural candidate for testing efficiency differentials.
While mean reversion has been studied extensively in developed global markets, the literature on emerging
markets — and India in particular — remains sparse. Even existing studies on Indian equities tend to focus
narrowly on the most liquid indices such as the NIFTY 50, leaving broader market segments largely
unexplored. The mid-cap segment, despite its growing economic significance and increasing share of
domestic equity investment, has received little empirical attention in the context of market efficiency.
Furthermore, the 2015–2024 study period encompasses multiple distinct market regimes — including a
prolonged bull market, the COVID-19 shock of 2020, and the subsequent recovery — providing a robust
cross-cyclical testing environment that single-regime studies cannot offer. This paper directly addresses
these gaps.
This paper empirically examines whether the theoretical prediction of greater mean reversion profitability in
less efficient segments holds, by applying a back tested mean reversion strategy to constituents of both
indices over the period 2015–2024 and comparing risk-adjusted returns as measured by the Sharpe Ratio.
The null hypothesis (H0) posits that mean reversion strategies do not generate statistically superior risk-
adjusted returns in Indian mid-cap equities relative to large-cap equities. The alternate hypothesis (H1)
posits that they do. The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews the existing
literature on EMH and mean reversion; Section 3 describes the data; Section 4 outlines the methodology;
Section 5 presents the results; Sections 6 and 7 discuss findings and limitations respectively; Section 8
concludes.

3. Data

3.1 Data Source
All stock price data used in this study was collected from Yahoo Finance, one of the most widely used
financial data platforms in academic research at the undergraduate and graduate level. The data was
accessed programmatically using the yfinance Python library — a well-documented, open-source tool that
provides a clean interface for retrieving historical price and volume data from Yahoo Finance&#39;s servers. This
approach allowed the entire dataset to be downloaded in a structured, reproducible manner, with each stock
saved as an individual comma-separated values (CSV) file.
The yfinance library was called with the auto adjust=True parameter enabled for every download. This is an
important technical detail: it instructs Yahoo Finance to return prices that have already been adjusted for
corporate actions, including stock splits, bonus share issuances, and dividend distributions. Without this
adjustment, a 2-for-1 stock split would appear in the raw data as a sudden 50% price drop overnight, which
has nothing to do with actual market behaviour. Adjusted prices eliminate these artefacts and ensure that the
daily return series for every stock is economically meaningful and comparable across the full ten-year
period. All price figures in this study — open, high, low, and close — therefore reflect adjusted values.

The study period runs from January 2015 to December 2024, spanning approximately 2,478 trading days per
stock. This ten-year window was a deliberate choice. It is long enough to capture multiple distinct market
regimes rather than a single favourable or unfavourable period. Specifically, the dataset encompasses: the
equity rally of 2015 through mid-2017; the disruption caused by the Indian government&#39;s demonetisation of
high-denomination currency in November 2016; the credit market stress that followed the IL&amp;FS default in
September 2018, which triggered a broad NBFC sector selloff; the COVID-19 shock in early 2020 and the
sharp recovery that followed; and the sustained broad-market bull run from 2021 through 2024, during
which mid-cap stocks in particular delivered substantial outperformance. Testing any return-based strategy
across a dataset that includes crashes, recoveries, liquidity crises, and bull markets is essential for drawing
conclusions that are not regime-specific.

3.2 Index Selection and Universe Definition
The selection of the NIFTY 100 and NIFTY Midcap 150 as the two universes for this study was not
arbitrary — it reflects a deliberate effort to compare two structurally distinct segments of the Indian equity
market using well-defined, publicly maintained benchmarks.
The NIFTY 100 is maintained by NSE Indices Limited, a wholly owned subsidiary of the National Stock
Exchange of India. These represent the top decile of listed Indian companies by market size and are
dominated by institutional ownership, analyst coverage, and high daily trading volumes. Large-cap stocks of
this kind are generally considered to be among the most efficiently priced in the Indian market, given the
volume of information that flows into their prices on a daily basis.
The NIFTY Midcap 150, also maintained by NSE Indices Limited, covers companies ranked 101 to 250 by
free-float market capitalisation on the NSE. These are meaningfully smaller companies than their NIFTY
100 counterparts — they attract fewer institutional investors, receive less analyst coverage, and trade with
lower daily liquidity. Retail investors constitute a proportionally larger share of the trading activity in this
segment. These structural characteristics are directly relevant to this study&#39;s hypothesis: if any segment of
the Indian equity market is likely to exhibit pricing inefficiencies that a mean reversion strategy can exploit,
it is the mid-cap segment, not the large-cap segment. The choice of these two indices therefore allows for a
direct, apples-to-apples comparison of return behaviour across the efficiency spectrum.
Constituent lists for both indices were sourced directly from the NSE website at the time of data collection.
It is important to note that these lists reflect current index composition, not historical composition — a point
discussed as a limitation in Section 6.

3.3 Sample Construction
The final sample used in this study consists of 96 large-cap stocks drawn from the NIFTY 100 and 142 mid-
cap stocks drawn from the NIFTY Midcap 150, giving a total of 238 stocks. The gap between these figures
and the 100 and 150 stocks that each index contains is entirely accounted for by the exclusions described in
the following subsection.
Two stocks are included in the sample but with shortened price histories due to recent IPOs. Bajaj Housing
Finance (BAJAJHFL) completed its initial public offering in September 2024 and Hyundai India
(HYUNDAI) listed in October 2024. Both are retained in the sample, with the understanding that their back
tested return contributions will reflect only the limited number of trading days available between their listing
dates and December 2024. This is not treated as a disqualifying limitation — it is standard practice in

empirical studies of this kind to include stocks from their available listing date rather than exclude them
entirely for having incomplete histories.
3.3.1 Exclusions
Twelve stocks that appear in the NSE constituent lists could not be included in the sample. Their exclusions
are documented in full in Table 1 below, along with the specific reason for each. A brief summary follows.
Eight stocks were excluded because they were listed on the NSE in 2025, after the study period ends in
December 2024. These are Anthem Biosciences, Groww (Nextbillion Technology), HDB Financial
Services, ICICI AMC, LG Electronics India, Lenskart, Tata Capital, and ITC Hotels. The last of these was
spun off from ITC Limited and began trading as an independent entity in January 2025 — while ITC itself
remains in the sample, ITC Hotels has no price history within the 2015–2024 window and cannot be
included. None of the eight represent a data retrieval failure; they simply do not exist as independently
traded instruments within the period being studied.
Hexaware Technologies (HEXT) presents a different case. The company was voluntarily delisted from
Indian exchanges in 2020 and only returned to the NSE in 2025 via a fresh listing. This means that a
continuous price series for the 2015–2024 period does not exist for this stock. Including partial pre-delisting
data was considered but rejected, as the structural break introduced by the delisting event would make the
series analytically inconsistent.
Enam Securities (ENRIN) was excluded due to the absence of a usable price series on Yahoo Finance.
Despite the stock being present on the NSE, no reliable historical data was available through the yfinance
interface for the study period.
Finally, Tata Motors DVR (TMPV) and its demerged counterpart (TMCV) were excluded due to the
structural complications arising from the Tata Motors corporate restructuring. Constructing a clean,
economically interpretable return series for either entity across the full study period is not feasible given the
demerger-related price discontinuities involved.

3.4 Data Collection Methodology
All data was downloaded using custom Python scripts written specifically for this project. Rather than
downloading all stocks in a single batch — which risks partial failures going undetected — each stock was
retrieved individually and saved as a separate CSV file in one of two designated folders: LARGECAPS/ for
NIFTY 100 stocks and MIDCAPS/ for NIFTY Midcap 150 stocks. This folder structure kept the two
universes clearly separated and made subsequent validation and back testing more straightforward to
manage.
Each CSV file contains six columns: date, open, high, low, close, and volume. The date column records each
trading day in the sample period, and the remaining columns record the corresponding adjusted daily price
and volume figures
After initial download, a systematic validation script was run across every file in both folders. This script
checked for four categories of data quality issues: missing date entries (gaps in the trading calendar), null or
NaN values in any price or volume column, zero-volume trading days, and extreme single-day price returns
exceeding a defined threshold. The purpose of this validation step was not to clean the data by removing
observations, but to document any anomalies so they could be handled explicitly during back testing rather
than silently distorting results.

3.5 Data Processing
Before the validation script was run, all CSV files underwent a normalisation step in which column headers
were converted to lowercase. This was a necessary pre-processing step because the back testing engine
references column names programmatically — any inconsistency in capitalisation between files would cause
silent failures or incorrect data reads. Lowercasing all headers ensured uniform compatibility across the
entire 238-stock dataset.
The validation process flagged two stocks for closer attention. GV Technologies and Distributors exhibited
isolated zero-volume entries on specific dates, most likely corresponding to trading halts or extremely
illiquid sessions where no transactions were recorded. Patanjali Foods showed one extreme single-day return
— a price movement of a magnitude that cannot plausibly reflect genuine market activity and is almost
certainly caused by a corporate action (such as a stock split or bonus issue) that was not fully reflected in the
adjusted price series at the time of download. Both cases have been documented. During back testing, such
observations will either be excluded from signal generation on the affected date or treated as non-trading
days, consistent with standard practice in empirical back testing.
No other material data quality issues were identified across the remaining 236 stocks. The dataset as a whole
is considered sufficiently clean for the purposes of this study.

3.6 Limitations of the Dataset

The most significant structural limitation of this dataset is survivorship bias. The NIFTY 100 and NIFTY
Midcap 150 constituent lists used for sample construction reflect the current composition of these indices,
not their composition at any historical point during the 2015–2024 study period. In practice, this means that
companies which were part of these indices at some point during the study window but were later dropped
— whether due to delisting, acquisition, sustained deterioration in market capitalisation, or index
reconstitution — are absent from this study&#39;s sample.
The consequence of this is that both the large-cap and mid-cap universes in this study consist, by
construction, of companies that have remained relevant and sufficiently large to retain their index
membership through to the present. Companies that failed, shrank significantly, or were removed from their
respective indices during the study period are not represented. This introduces an upward bias in measured
performance across both universes: the stocks being studied are, in a sense, the survivors. Mean reversion
strategies applied to a universe of survivors may appear to perform better than they would have performed in
real time, when the index composition included companies that subsequently declined or were removed.
This limitation is real and cannot be fully corrected for without access to historical index constituent data,
which is not publicly available for the full 2015–2024 period at the individual stock level. It is therefore
treated as a documented limitation of this study rather than a methodological flaw, and its implications for
interpreting the results are discussed in Section 6.

3.7 Descriptive Statistics
Table 2 reports summary statistics for both universes across the full sample period. Metrics reported include
mean daily return, annualised volatility (computed as the standard deviation of daily returns scaled by the
square root of 252 trading days), skewness, and excess kurtosis. These figures provide a baseline
characterisation of each universe before any strategy is applied and allow for a direct comparison of the
distributional properties of large-cap and mid-cap returns.
Differences in volatility and tail behaviour between the two groups are particularly relevant to the central
hypothesis of this study. Higher volatility and fatter tails in the mid-cap universe would be consistent with
the theoretical argument that these stocks are priced less efficiently — that is, that prices deviate more
substantially from fundamental value and therefore present greater scope for mean reversion to be detectable
and exploitable.
